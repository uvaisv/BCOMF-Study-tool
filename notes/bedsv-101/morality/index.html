Morality & Theories of Conduct

Critical Asked in 4 of 4 papers · avg 20 marks

Facets of morality, the 'good' and self-realisation, rules and theories of conduct.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 1 · Unit 2 Concept of Morality and Moral EducationBlock 1 · Unit 3 Dimensions of MoralityBlock 3 · Unit 1 Theories of GoodnessBlock 3 · Unit 2 Theories of ConductBlock 3 · Unit 3 Reason and Emotions in Human Conduct

Key points

  • Moral consciousness is the human sense of right and wrong and of caring for others.
  • Moral behaviour has a form (it must be rational and universalisable) and a content (the actual act). Moral instruction, training and indoctrination are not the same as moral education.
  • Criteria of moral judgement: the intention behind the act and free will — an act done without free will is neither moral nor immoral; an act done with good will is morally good whatever its result.
  • Three facets of morality in the book: caring (for others), judging (the situation — Piaget and Kohlberg stress rules and universal principles; Gilligan stresses care) and acting. Without all three, behaviour is not moral.
  • Theories of goodness: ethical naturalism (moral words can be put in non-moral terms, e.g. 'good' = what I approve), emotive theory (moral statements only express feelings), intrinsic vs instrumental good, hedonism (pleasure is the good), ethical pluralism (many things are good in themselves — knowledge, good will, loyalty) and self-realisation.
  • Theories of conduct: universalisability (Kant's categorical imperative), ethical egoism and rational self-interest, the general good theory (act utilitarianism), rule utilitarianism, and non-consequential duties (gratitude, fidelity, justice).
  • Reason and emotion work together in morality: instead of repressing children's emotions, teachers should educate them (love, care, empathy). Moral weakness is knowing what is right but not doing it.

Worked examples

Example: intention, free will and the three facets

Two students both return a lost wallet. One does it out of concern for the owner; the other only because a camera was watching. The act is the same, but the intention differs — and moral judgement looks at the intention.

Caring (feeling for the owner), judging (deciding that returning it is right) and acting (actually returning it) must all be present for fully moral behaviour.

Example: theories of conduct applied

Should a company tell customers about a minor defect? Kant's universalisability: if every firm hid defects, no one could trust any product — so disclose. Act utilitarianism: weigh total happiness in this one case. Rule utilitarianism: follow the rule 'always disclose defects', because that rule produces the most good overall.

How to write the answer

Meaning → each theory with its main idea, a thinker and one example → criticism in one line → conclusion.

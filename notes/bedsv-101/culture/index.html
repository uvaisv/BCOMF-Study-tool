Indian Culture, Philosophy & Cultural Pluralism

Critical Asked in 4 of 4 papers · avg 15 marks

Values of Indian culture, schools of Indian philosophy, cultural pluralism and its challenges.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 2 · Unit 1 Indian Culture and Human ValuesBlock 2 · Unit 2 Values Enshrined in Indian PhilosophyBlock 2 · Unit 3 Cultural Pluralism in Indian Society

Key points

  • Indian culture is over 5,000 years old and has survived by absorbing and assimilating many influences; despite its diversity it has a 'fundamental unity'.
  • Values carried by Indian culture through family, religion, festivals, food and clothes, music, dance, art, language and national symbols: sharing, caring, tolerance, unity, loyalty, integrity and concern for others.
  • Four key concepts of Indian philosophy: dharma, karma, moksha and atman.
  • Classical schools: six āstika (orthodox) — Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Mimamsa, Vedanta; nāstika (heterodox) — Buddhism, Jainism, Charvaka. Vedanta is still a 'living school'.
  • Later strands: the Bhakti movement (devotion over ritual), Sikhism (Guru Nanak), Sufism (direct experience of God through a pir), and modern thinkers such as Swami Vivekananda (Ramakrishna Mission, 1897).
  • Values from Indian philosophy: tolerance, forgiveness, devotion, scientific temper, non-violence, truth, humility and detachment.
  • Cultural pluralism: many cultures living together while keeping their identity. Its values: acceptance, compassion, equality, tolerance, appreciation and adjustment. Tagore, Gandhi, Nehru and Vivekananda championed it.
  • Challenge: globalisation tends to make cultures uniform; we must keep our culture and its diversity.

Worked example

Example: cultural pluralism in a housing colony

Families from Kerala, Punjab and Assam live in one colony. They celebrate Onam, Lohri and Bihu together, share food and learn each other's festivals, while each family keeps its own language and customs. This is pluralism — acceptance, appreciation and adjustment without losing identity — unlike assimilation, where everyone is expected to become the same.

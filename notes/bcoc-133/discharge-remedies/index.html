Discharge, Breach, Remedies & Quasi Contracts

Critical Asked in 11 of 11 papers · avg 17 marks

Modes of discharge, supervening impossibility, breach and remedies (damages, specific performance, injunction, quantum meruit), quasi and contingent contracts.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 2 · Unit 7 Performance and DischargeBlock 2 · Unit 8 Remedies for Breach and Quasi ContractsBlock 2 · Unit 6 Void Agreements and Contingent Contracts (6.3 Contingent contracts)

Key points

  • Discharge by: performance, mutual agreement (novation, rescission, alteration, remission, waiver), impossibility, lapse of time, operation of law (death, insolvency, merger), breach.
  • Supervening impossibility (Sec 56): contract becomes void if performance becomes impossible later — destruction of subject matter, death or incapacity, change of law, war. Not covered: difficulty, commercial hardship, strikes, failure of third party.
  • Breach: actual or anticipatory.
  • Remedies: rescission, damages (ordinary, special, exemplary, nominal — Hadley v Baxendale remoteness rule), specific performance (where money is not adequate, e.g. unique property), injunction, quantum meruit (payment for work done).
  • Quasi contracts (Secs 68–72): necessaries supplied to an incapable person, payment by an interested person, benefit of a non-gratuitous act, finder of goods, money paid by mistake or coercion.
  • Contingent contract (Sec 31): performance depends on an uncertain future event; enforceable only when the event happens.
  • Performance: the promisor, their agent or legal representatives must perform; performance can be demanded by the promisee (or their representatives). Where time is of the essence, delay lets the other party avoid the contract.

Worked examples

Example: remoteness of damage (Hadley v Baxendale)

A mill's broken crankshaft was sent through a carrier to be copied, but delivery was delayed and the mill stayed shut longer. The mill owners claimed their lost profits. The carrier had not been told the mill would stand idle without the shaft, so that loss was too remote: damages cover only losses arising naturally from the breach, or losses both parties knew of when contracting.

Example: supervening impossibility

A singer agrees to perform in a hall on a set date; the hall is destroyed by fire before then. Performance has become impossible, so the contract is void (Section 56) and neither side is liable.

But if costs rise sharply because of a transport strike, the contract is not discharged — mere difficulty or hardship is not impossibility.

Example: quasi contract

A trader delivers goods to the wrong shop by mistake and the shopkeeper uses them, knowing they were not a gift. There was no contract, but the shopkeeper must pay for them — benefit of a non-gratuitous act (Section 70).

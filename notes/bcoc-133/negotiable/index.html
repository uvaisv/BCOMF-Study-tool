Negotiable Instruments

Critical Asked in 11 of 11 papers · avg 21 marks

Promissory note, bill of exchange, cheque, endorsement, crossing, holder in due course, presentment, lost/stolen/forged instruments.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 6 · Unit 20 Negotiable Instruments and Its PartiesBlock 6 · Unit 21 Promissory Note, Bills of Exchange and ChequeBlock 6 · Unit 22 NegotiationBlock 6 · Unit 23 Presentment and Discharge

Key points

  • Negotiable instrument: promissory note, bill of exchange or cheque payable to order or bearer (Sec 13). Features: freely transferable, holder in due course gets good title, presumptions of consideration and date.
  • Promissory note (Sec 4): unconditional written promise by the maker to pay. Bill of exchange (Sec 5): unconditional written order to pay; three parties — drawer, drawee, payee. Cheque (Sec 6): bill drawn on a banker, payable on demand.
  • Holder vs holder in due course (Sec 9): HDC took it for value, before maturity, in good faith — gets a better title than the transferor.
  • Endorsement: signing to transfer. Kinds: blank, special/full, restrictive, partial, conditional, sans recourse, facultative.
  • Crossing: general (two parallel lines, pay through a bank), special (bank named), account payee, not negotiable.
  • Presentment for acceptance (bills payable after sight) and for payment: at proper place, time, by the holder.
  • Negotiation (by delivery/endorsement, HDC gets good title, no notice) vs assignment (by written document, assignee gets transferor's title, notice needed).
  • Lost or stolen instrument: the finder gets no title, but an HDC from the finder does; forged instrument: nobody gets title through a forged endorsement.
  • A bank may refuse a cheque: insufficient funds, stale (over 3 months), post-dated, mutilated, signature mismatch, stop-payment order, account closed, insolvency or death of the customer.
  • Inchoate instrument: signed stamped paper left blank — holder may complete it. Hundi: indigenous negotiable instrument.
  • Maturity: three days of grace are added to notes and bills not payable on demand. If the due date is a public holiday, the Act (Sec 25) makes the instrument due on the next preceding business day — the study material says 'next business day', but the Act's wording is 'preceding' (Secs 22–25).
  • Dishonour by non-acceptance: the drawee does not accept within 48 hours, gives a qualified acceptance, cannot be found, is incompetent, fictitious, dead or insolvent. Dishonour by non-payment: the party liable fails to pay when it is duly presented.
  • Discharge of a party (the instrument remains negotiable) vs discharge of the instrument (all rights end). Modes include cancellation, release, payment in due course and material alteration without consent.

Worked examples

Example: working out the date of maturity

A bill payable 30 days after sight is presented for sight on 1 March. Counting 30 days, excluding the day of presentment, gives 31 March; adding three days of grace makes it due on 3 April.

If the due date is a public holiday, Section 25 makes it due on the next preceding business day.

Example: who gets title?

A thief steals a bearer cheque and passes it for value to C, who takes it in good faith before maturity. C is a holder in due course and gets a good title — the owner cannot recover the amount from C.

If the cheque were payable to order and the thief forged the payee's endorsement, nobody would get a title through the forged signature.

Example: crossings

A cheque with two parallel lines is crossed generally — paid only through a bank, not over the counter. Two lines with 'State Bank of India' written between them is a special crossing, paid only through that bank. 'Account payee' means the collecting bank must credit the money only to the payee's account.

Budgets & Budgetary Control

Critical Asked in 9 of 9 papers · avg 23 marks

Budgeting and budgetary control, objectives, advantages and limitations, types of budgets, flexible budget, cash budget, master and sales budgets, zero-based and performance budgeting, control ratios.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 2 · Unit 5 Budgeting: An OverviewBlock 2 · Unit 6 Preparation of BudgetsBlock 2 · Unit 7 Approaches to BudgetingBlock 2 · Unit 8 Budgetary Control

Key points

  • Budget: a quantitative plan for a future period. Budgetary control: setting budgets, comparing actuals with them and acting on differences.
  • Objectives and advantages: planning, coordination, control, fixing responsibility, efficient use of resources, motivation. Limitations: based on estimates, rigid, costly, needs support of staff.
  • Types: by time (long-term, short-term), by function (sales, production, materials, labour, overhead, cash, capital expenditure), by flexibility (fixed, flexible); master budget combines all functional budgets.
  • Flexible budget: shows costs at different levels of activity by separating fixed, variable and semi-variable costs. Steps: decide activity levels, classify costs, compute each at every level, find the overhead rate.
  • Cash budget: estimates cash receipts and payments to find surpluses or shortages. Methods: receipts and payments (short term), adjusted profit and loss (start from profit, add non-cash items, adjust working capital and capital items — long term), balance sheet method.
  • Zero-based budgeting: every activity is justified from zero each year rather than adding to last year's budget — cuts waste, links spending to priorities.
  • Performance budgeting: budgets linked to programmes, activities and measurable outputs; process — define programmes, set targets and performance measures, allocate funds, review results.
  • Control ratios: capacity ratio = actual hours ÷ budgeted hours; activity ratio = standard hours for actual output ÷ budgeted hours; efficiency ratio = standard hours ÷ actual hours.

Worked examples

Example: flexible budget

Capacity 10,000 units. Variable cost ₹20 a unit; semi-variable ₹20,000 fixed + ₹3 a unit; fixed ₹1,00,000.

At 60% (6,000 units): 1,20,000 + 38,000 + 1,00,000 = ₹2,58,000 → ₹43 a unit.

At 80% (8,000 units): 1,60,000 + 44,000 + 1,00,000 = ₹3,04,000 → ₹38 a unit.

At 100% (10,000 units): 2,00,000 + 50,000 + 1,00,000 = ₹3,50,000 → ₹35 a unit — unit cost falls as fixed costs are spread.

Example: a month of a cash budget, and control ratios

Opening cash ₹20,000 + cash sales 30,000 + collection of last month's credit sales 50,000 − paid to creditors 40,000 − wages 15,000 − rent 5,000 − machine 30,000 = closing ₹10,000.

Control ratios: budgeted hours 10,000; actual hours 9,000; standard hours for actual output 9,900.

Capacity = 9,000/10,000 = 90%; activity = 9,900/10,000 = 99%; efficiency = 9,900/9,000 = 110%. (Activity = capacity × efficiency: 0.9 × 1.1 = 0.99.)

How to solve

  1. Classify each cost as fixed, variable or semi-variable (split semi-variable into fixed and variable).
  2. Variable cost at each level = rate per unit × units; fixed stays the same.
  3. Total the costs at each level; divide by units or hours for the rate.
  4. Cash budget: opening balance + receipts − payments = closing balance, month by month (with time lags for debtors and creditors).

Formulas

Capacity ratio = Actual hours ÷ Budgeted hours × 100
Activity ratio = Standard hours for actual output ÷ Budgeted hours × 100
Efficiency ratio = Standard hours ÷ Actual hours × 100

How to write the answer

Budget in columns for each activity level; state fixed, variable and semi-variable items separately.

Common mistakes

  • Keeping fixed costs per unit constant across levels — fixed cost in total is constant.

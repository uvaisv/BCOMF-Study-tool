Standard Costing & Variance Analysis

Critical Asked in 9 of 9 papers · avg 20 marks

Meaning, objectives, advantages and limitations of standard costing; standard costing vs budgeting and estimate costing; material and labour variances.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 3 · Unit 9 Standard Costing: An OverviewBlock 3 · Unit 10 Material VariancesBlock 3 · Unit 11 Labour VariancesBlock 3 · Unit 12 Overhead Variances

Key points

  • Standard costing: setting predetermined costs for each element, comparing actual costs with them and analysing the differences (variances) for control.
  • Objectives and advantages: cost control, measuring efficiency, fixing responsibility, management by exception, pricing and budgeting help, cost consciousness, simpler stock valuation.
  • Limitations: setting accurate standards is hard, standards date quickly, costly for small firms, may demotivate if too tight, not suited to job work.
  • Standard costing vs budgeting: unit-level vs total; mainly production costs vs all functions; a technique of cost control vs a planning tool; variances analysed by cause vs totals compared.
  • Estimate costing vs standard costing: estimates are guesses of what costs will be (for quotations), standards are what costs should be (for control), set scientifically.
  • Material variances: cost = price + usage; usage = mix + yield (or sub-usage). Labour variances: cost = rate + efficiency (+ idle time); efficiency = mix + sub-efficiency.
  • Overhead variances (Unit 12): variable overhead — expenditure and efficiency; fixed overhead — expenditure and volume (volume = capacity + efficiency, plus calendar). Sales variances: price and volume (volume = mix + quantity).

Worked examples

Example: material variances

Standard: 2 kg per unit at ₹10 a kg. Actual output 1,000 units, using 2,100 kg bought at ₹9.50.

Standard quantity for actual output = 2,000 kg; standard cost ₹20,000; actual cost ₹19,950.

MCV = 20,000 − 19,950 = ₹50 (F).

MPV = 2,100 × (10 − 9.50) = ₹1,050 (F).

MUV = 10 × (2,000 − 2,100) = ₹1,000 (A).

Check: 1,050 F − 1,000 A = 50 F ✓. Buying cheaper saved money, but more material was wasted.

Example: labour variances

Standard: 3 hours per unit at ₹20 an hour. For 1,000 units, 2,900 hours are worked at ₹21.

Standard hours = 3,000; standard cost ₹60,000; actual cost ₹60,900 → LCV ₹900 (A).

LRV = 2,900 × (20 − 21) = ₹2,900 (A). LEV = 20 × (3,000 − 2,900) = ₹2,000 (F).

Check: 2,900 A − 2,000 F = 900 A ✓. Workers were paid more but worked faster.

Example: material mix variance

Standard mix: A 60% at ₹10, B 40% at ₹15. Actual input 200 kg: A 110 kg, B 90 kg.

Revised standard quantity (200 kg in the standard ratio): A 120, B 80.

MMV = 10 × (120 − 110) + 15 × (80 − 90) = 100 − 150 = ₹50 (A) — more of the costlier material B was used.

How to solve

  1. Find standard quantity (or hours) for actual output: scale the standard to the actual output or yield.
  2. Material cost variance = standard cost of actual output − actual cost.
  3. Price variance = AQ × (SP − AP); usage variance = SP × (SQ − AQ).
  4. Mix variance = SP × (revised standard quantity − AQ), where RSQ = actual total input in the standard ratio.
  5. Yield variance = standard cost per unit of output × (actual yield − standard yield from actual input).
  6. For labour, repeat with hours and rates; check rate + efficiency = cost variance.

Formulas

MCV = (SQ × SP) − (AQ × AP)
MPV = AQ × (SP − AP)
MUV = SP × (SQ − AQ)
MMV = SP × (RSQ − AQ)
LCV = (SH × SR) − (AH × AR)
LRV = AH × (SR − AR)
LEV = SR × (SH − AH)

How to write the answer

Working table (SQ, SP, AQ, AP) → each variance with formula and F/A → check that parts add up to the total.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to mark each variance favourable (F) or adverse (A).
  • Using standard quantity for total input instead of standard quantity for actual output.

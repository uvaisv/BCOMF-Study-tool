Monopolistic Competition & Oligopoly

Critical Asked in 11 of 11 papers · avg 15 marks

Excess capacity, kinked demand curve, Cournot and Stackelberg models, prisoner's dilemma.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 4 · Unit 11 Monopolistic Competition: Price and Output DecisionsBlock 4 · Unit 12 Oligopoly: Price and Output Decisions

Key points

  • Monopolistic competition: many firms, differentiated products, free entry; long-run tangency of AR with LAC on its falling part → excess capacity.
  • Kinked demand curve (Sweezy): rivals match price cuts but not rises → kink at current price, gap in MR → price rigidity. Limitation: does not explain how the price was set.
  • Cournot: each firm chooses quantity taking rival's output as given; equilibrium where reaction curves cross.
  • Stackelberg: leader moves first knowing the follower's reaction — first-mover advantage.
  • Prisoner's dilemma: each player's dominant strategy leads to a worse joint outcome (e.g. cartel cheating).
  • Oligopoly: a few interdependent firms. Firms may behave non-cooperatively (Cournot, Stackelberg) or cooperate.
  • Cartel: firms agree jointly on output and price to act like a monopolist (e.g. OPEC); each member is tempted to cheat by producing more, which makes cartels unstable.

Worked examples

Example: Cournot vs Stackelberg vs monopoly

Market demand P = 100 − Q; each firm's marginal cost ₹10.

Cournot: each firm makes (100 − 10)/3 = 30; total 60; price ₹40.

Stackelberg: the leader makes 90/2 = 45, the follower 90/4 = 22.5; total 67.5; price ₹32.50 — the leader gains by moving first.

If the two formed a cartel and acted as one monopolist: total 45, price ₹55. Under perfect competition: price ₹10, output 90.

Example: prisoner's dilemma in a cartel

Two cement firms agree to restrict output and each earn ₹10 crore. If one secretly produces more while the other keeps to the deal, the cheat earns ₹14 crore and the other ₹4 crore. Whatever the other does, cheating pays more, so both cheat and each earns ₹6 crore — worse for both than sticking to the deal. This is why cartels tend to break down.

How to solve

  1. Cournot (linear demand P = a − bQ, equal cost c): each firm q = (a − c)/(3b).
  2. Stackelberg: leader q₁ = (a − c)/(2b); follower q₂ = (a − c)/(4b).

Formulas

Cournot: q = (a − c)/3b each
Stackelberg: q₁ = (a − c)/2b, q₂ = (a − c)/4b

Common mistakes

  • Drawing the kinked MR without the vertical gap.

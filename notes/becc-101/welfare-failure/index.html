Welfare, Market Failure & Externalities

High Asked in 10 of 11 papers · avg 11 marks

Pareto efficiency and welfare theorems, sources of market failure, externalities, public goods, asymmetric information.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 6 · Unit 16 Welfare: Allocative Efficiency under Perfect CompetitionBlock 6 · Unit 17 Efficiency of the Market Mechanism: Market Failure and the Role of the State

Key points

  • Pareto efficiency: no one can be made better off without making someone worse off; needs efficiency in exchange, production and product mix.
  • First fundamental theorem: a competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient.
  • Sources of market failure: market power, externalities, public goods, incomplete/asymmetric information.
  • Externality: a cost or benefit to third parties. Remedies: Pigouvian tax (negative) or subsidy (positive), regulation, tradable permits, Coase bargaining.
  • Public goods: non-rival and non-excludable (e.g. street lights) → free-rider problem. Merit goods: under-consumed if left to the market (e.g. education).
  • Asymmetric information: adverse selection (before the deal — Akerlof's 'lemons') and moral hazard (after the deal — insured drivers taking more risk).
  • With an externality, marginal social cost = marginal private cost + marginal external cost (MSC = MPC + MEC); the market over-produces goods with external costs.
  • Government remedies in the book: Pigouvian taxes and subsidies, direct regulation (price ceilings on stents and essential medicines, safety standards, pollution permits, advertising bans) and private bargaining.

Worked examples

Example: pollution and a Pigouvian tax

Demand for a chemical: P = 100 − Q. A factory's private marginal cost is ₹40 a unit, but each unit causes ₹10 of pollution damage, so MSC = ₹50.

The market produces where P = MPC: Q = 60. The social optimum is where P = MSC: Q = 50. The extra 10 units create a deadweight loss of ½ × 10 × 10 = ₹50.

A tax of ₹10 a unit (equal to the external cost) makes the factory produce 50.

Example: asymmetric information

Adverse selection: in a used-car market, sellers know which cars are 'lemons' but buyers do not, so buyers offer an average price; owners of good cars withdraw, and mostly lemons are left.

Moral hazard: after buying full theft insurance, a person stops locking their bike carefully.

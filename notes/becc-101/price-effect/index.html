Income, Substitution & Price Effects

Critical Asked in 9 of 11 papers · avg 15 marks

Splitting the price effect (Hicks and Slutsky), inferior and Giffen goods, deriving demand curves.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 2 · Unit 5 Consumer Behaviour: Ordinal Approach (5.9–5.10)

Key points

  • Price effect = substitution effect + income effect.
  • Substitution effect is always negative (buy more of the cheaper good).
  • Normal good: income effect reinforces substitution effect. Inferior good: income effect works against it but is weaker. Giffen good: income effect is stronger, so demand rises with price.
  • Hicks: compensating variation keeps the consumer on the original indifference curve.
  • Slutsky: cost-difference keeps the original bundle affordable (consumer ends on a higher curve).
  • Demand curve derived from the price consumption curve (money income constant) or with compensated income (real income constant — Hicksian demand).

Worked example

Example: splitting a price effect

The price of tea falls from ₹20 to ₹10 a cup and a consumer's purchases rise from 4 to 7 cups a week (price effect +3).

Take away just enough income to put them back on their old indifference curve (Hicks): they would buy 6 cups → substitution effect +2. The remaining +1 is the income effect → tea is a normal good.

If instead the income effect were −1 (total +1), tea would be inferior; if it were −3 (total −1, buying less at a lower price), it would be a Giffen good.

How to write the answer

Common mistakes

  • Drawing the compensated budget line not parallel to the new one.

Data, Variables, Scales & Sources

Moderate Asked in 5 of 8 papers · avg 7 marks

Meaning of statistics and data, qualitative and quantitative variables, discrete and continuous variables, measurement scales, types of data (time series, cross-section, pooled, panel), primary and secondary sources, and sources and limitations of secondary data.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 1 · Unit 2 Statistical Concepts (2.2)Block 2 · Unit 4 Data Collection: Methods and Sources (4.7 Secondary data)

Key points

  • Statistics (singular) means the methods of collecting, organising, analysing and interpreting data; (plural) the numerical data themselves.
  • Qualitative variables are categories (gender, religion); quantitative variables are numbers — discrete (counted: children in a family) or continuous (measured: height, temperature). An explanatory (independent) variable is used to explain or predict a response (dependent) variable.
  • Measurement scales: nominal (named categories, no order — gender), ordinal (ordered categories, unequal gaps — good/average/poor), interval (equal gaps, no true zero — temperature in °C, IQ), ratio (equal gaps and a true zero — height, income). The scale decides which statistics can be used.
  • Types of data: time series (one variable over time — GDP by year), cross-section (many units at one time — Census 2011), pooled (both combined), panel or longitudinal (the same units followed over time).
  • Primary data: collected first-hand for the purpose; costly but specific. Secondary data: collected by others; cheaper and quicker but may not fit the purpose.
  • Sources of secondary data in India: MoSPI (Statistical Abstract), Census reports, NSSO surveys, Annual Survey of Industries, RBI (Handbook of Statistics, bulletins, DBIE), Economic Survey, Budget documents, Labour Bureau, CMIE and EPWRF databases, international bodies (UN, World Bank, ILO).
  • Limitations of secondary data: may not suit the purpose, different definitions and units, may be outdated, unknown accuracy, bias of the original collector. Before use, record who collected it, when, how, the title, edition and publisher.
  • Quantitative data are summarised by tables, graphs, averages, dispersion, skewness and correlation; qualitative data by frequencies, percentages, the mode and by coding themes.

Worked examples

Examples: classify the variable and its scale

Gender → qualitative, nominal. Satisfaction (high/medium/low) → qualitative, ordinal. Temperature in °C → quantitative, interval (no true zero). Monthly income → quantitative, ratio, continuous. Number of children → quantitative, discrete.

For a study of the share of women doing home-based work (yes/no) the data are nominal, so the right average is the mode (most common category), reported with percentages.

Examples: types and sources of data

India's GDP for 2000–2025 → time series. Literacy of all states in Census 2011 → cross-section. The same 2,000 households surveyed every year → panel. A researcher using NSSO unit data or the RBI Handbook → secondary data; first record who collected it, when, how and with which definitions.

How to write the answer

Definitions → classification (variables, scales or sources) with an example each → suitability or limitations → conclusion.

Common mistakes

  • Calling temperature in °C a ratio scale (it has no true zero).
  • Confusing cross-section with time-series data.

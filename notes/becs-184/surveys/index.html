Census, Sample Surveys & Errors

High Asked in 7 of 8 papers · avg 16 marks

Census and sample survey, population, frame and sampling fraction, random and non-random sampling, stages in planning and organising an enquiry, sampling and non-sampling errors, cost of an enquiry and choosing between a census and a survey.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 2 · Unit 4 Data Collection: Methods and Sources

Key points

  • Population (universe): all units under study; a list of the units is the frame (sampling frame). A census enumerates every unit (e.g. India's Population Census, the Economic Census); a sample survey covers a selected subset (e.g. NSSO surveys, Annual Survey of Industries).
  • Sampling fraction = sample size ÷ population size.
  • Random sampling gives every unit a known chance of selection, so conclusions about the population and the size of the sampling error can be worked out. Non-random sampling (convenience, purposive, snowball, quota) gives no such assurance; it suits exploratory work.
  • Stages in planning and organising a census or survey: (1) scope and objectives with a tabulation plan, (2) preparation of the frame, (3) finalising the schedule or questionnaire, (4) manual of instructions and training of field staff, (5) scrutiny note and coding plan, (6) recruitment and manpower planning, (7) organisation of fieldwork.
  • Organising fieldwork: allot workloads to investigators and supervisors, keep forms and stationery supplied, answer field queries quickly, keep a leave reserve, and dispatch filled schedules safely for processing.
  • Sampling error: the difference between the sample result and the true population value that arises because only a sample is studied; it can be estimated and controlled (by sample size) only in random samples.
  • Non-sampling errors (in census and survey alike): errors of measurement, false or cooked-up data, investigator and respondent bias, recall lapse, faulty or incomplete frame, non-response, and processing and presentation errors. Remedies: clear definitions, training, supervision, cross-checks, short reference periods, an up-to-date frame.
  • Why a sample survey may be preferred to a census: lower cost, faster results, can be repeated more often, easier to organise, better training and supervision so fewer non-sampling errors, more detailed data. A census is needed when figures are required for every small area or unit.
  • Cost and duration depend on: census or survey, the number and spread of units, the amount of data per unit, and the mode of collection (visit, mail, phone, records).

Worked examples

Example: planning a household consumption survey

Scope and objectives with a tabulation plan (expenditure by item and income group) → frame (the latest census house list) → schedule (items, reference period 30 days) → instruction manual and training (what counts as consumption) → scrutiny note and coding plan → recruitment of investigators and supervisors → fieldwork (workloads, supplies, quick answers to queries, safe dispatch of schedules).

With 400 households sampled from 20,000, the sampling fraction is 400 ÷ 20,000 = 0.02 (2%).

Example: spotting non-sampling errors

A respondent rounds her age to 40 (measurement error); an investigator fills a form without visiting (cooked-up data); a husband answers for his wife about her work (respondent bias); a farmer forgets last year's illness (recall lapse); new houses are missing from an old list (faulty frame); locked houses are skipped (non-response). Asking people outside a mall is convenience (non-random) sampling: cheap and quick, but its results cannot be generalised, and the sampling error cannot be measured.

Diagram

How to write the answer

Definitions (census, survey, frame) → stages or types of errors as headed points with remedies → comparison or conclusion.

Common mistakes

  • Saying non-sampling errors occur only in sample surveys — they occur in censuses too, often more.
  • Calling convenience sampling 'random'.

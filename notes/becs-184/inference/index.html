Normal Distribution, Estimation & Sampling Concepts

High Asked in 8 of 8 papers · avg 14 marks

Exploratory data analysis and boxplots, parameters and statistics, random variables and probability distributions, the normal and standard normal distributions, central limit theorem, standard error, point and interval estimation, properties of a good estimator and the t distribution.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 3 · Unit 7 Univariate Data Analysis

Key points

  • Descriptive statistics summarise the data in hand; inferential statistics use a sample to draw conclusions about a population, with a measured risk of error.
  • Parameter: a numerical summary of the population (μ, σ), usually unknown. Statistic: the same summary computed from a sample (X̄, s), used to estimate the parameter.
  • Random variable: a variable whose value depends on chance (number of heads in 3 tosses, daily rainfall). A probability distribution lists its values with their probabilities; for a continuous variable, probabilities are areas under the curve, the curve is never negative and the total area is 1.
  • Normal distribution: bell-shaped, symmetrical, unimodal; mean = median = mode at the centre; continuous; tails never touch the x-axis; total area 1; about 68%, 95% and 99.7% of values lie within 1, 2 and 3 SDs of the mean.
  • Standard normal distribution: mean 0, SD 1; any normal value is converted by z = (X − μ) ÷ σ and areas read from the z table — used to find probabilities and percentages.
  • Central limit theorem: the distribution of the sample mean approaches normal as n grows (n ≥ 30 is usually enough), whatever the shape of the population. Its standard deviation is the standard error = σ ÷ √n.
  • Point estimate: a single value (sample mean 65). Interval estimate (confidence interval): a range believed to contain the parameter with a stated confidence, e.g. 95% CI = X̄ ± 1.96 σ/√n. Higher confidence gives a wider interval.
  • A good estimator is unbiased (its expected value equals the parameter), consistent (approaches the parameter as n grows) and efficient (has the smallest variance).
  • t distribution: bell-shaped and symmetric about 0 like the normal, but flatter with heavier tails; a family of curves indexed by degrees of freedom (n − 1); approaches the normal as d.f. increase. Used for small samples when σ is unknown.
  • Boxplot (five-number summary: minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum) shows the centre, spread, skewness and outliers at a glance.

Worked examples

Example: normal distribution and a confidence interval

Marks are normal with mean 50 and SD 10. A student scoring 70 has z = (70 − 50) ÷ 10 = 2, so about 97.7% of students scored below them. About 95% of students lie between 30 and 70 (mean ± 2 SD).

A sample of 100 students has mean marks 65 with σ = 10: standard error = 10 ÷ √100 = 1, so the 95% CI = 65 ± 1.96 × 1 = 63.04 to 66.96.

Example: parameter vs statistic, and degrees of freedom

The average income of all households in a city (μ) is a parameter; the average of 500 sampled households (X̄ = ₹32,000) is a statistic used to estimate it.

Degrees of freedom: if 5 values must average 10 (total 50), four can be anything, but the fifth is then fixed — so d.f. = n − 1 = 4.

Formulas

z = (X − μ) ÷ σ
Standard error = σ ÷ √n
95% CI for μ = X̄ ± 1.96 σ/√n
t = (X̄ − μ) ÷ (s/√n), d.f. = n − 1

How to write the answer

Definition → properties or comparison as numbered points → a small numerical or diagram illustration → uses → conclusion.

Common mistakes

  • Saying the normal curve touches the x-axis.
  • Confusing the standard deviation of the data with the standard error of the mean.
  • Thinking a 95% interval contains 95% of the data values.

Tools & Methods of Data Collection

Critical Asked in 7 of 8 papers · avg 18 marks

Primary and secondary data collection: census, survey, observation and experimental methods; quantitative tools (questionnaire, schedule, interview) and qualitative tools (participant observation, focused interview, oral and life histories, case study, group and focus group discussions, key informants, narratives).

In your IGNOU bookBlock 2 · Unit 4 Data Collection: Methods and Sources (4.2 Methods)Block 2 · Unit 5 Tools of Data Collection

Key points

  • Primary data are collected first-hand for the study; secondary data were collected by someone else (census reports, NSSO, RBI, Economic Survey, CMIE). Four methods of collecting primary data: census, sample survey, observation and experiment.
  • Observation records events as they happen (temperature, blood pressure); the experimental method controls other factors to measure the effect of one (e.g. manure on yield).
  • Quantitative research: one reality, researcher separate from the subject, large representative samples, testing hypotheses (deductive). Its tools: questionnaire, schedule, structured interview, observation.
  • Questionnaire (filled by the respondent) vs schedule (filled by the investigator, who asks the questions). A good questionnaire: covering letter, short, simple language, no personal or sensitive questions, no double negatives, logical order from general to specific, no leading questions, neat layout.
  • Interviews: structured (fixed questions, comparable answers — for descriptive studies) or unstructured (flexible, open questions — for exploratory and qualitative research; rich insight but costly, needs skill, risk of bias, answers hard to compare).
  • Qualitative research: many socially constructed realities, researcher close to the subject, small purposive samples, inductive. Its tools: participant observation, focused (in-depth) interview, oral and life histories, case study, group discussion, focus group discussion, key informants, narratives.
  • Participant observation: the researcher lives and works with the group — natural, detailed data; but not standardised and the researcher may lose objectivity. Non-participant or quasi-participant observation (watching from outside, video) reduces these drawbacks.
  • Case study (Yin): an in-depth study of one unit (firm, village) in its real-life context, using several sources of evidence — holistic, inductive and idiographic. Focus group discussion: a planned discussion of 6–10 people led by a moderator to learn group norms and meanings.
  • Key informants: people who know the community well (officials, health workers, leaders, shopkeepers). Narratives: letting respondents tell their own stories, since different people read the same question differently.

Worked examples

Example: five questions for a Swachh Bharat study (Dec 2024 Q10)

1. Does your household have its own toilet? (Yes/No)

2. If yes, do all members use it regularly? (Always/Sometimes/Never)

3. How is household waste disposed of? (Collected by panchayat/Dumped in open/Burnt/Other)

4. Has anyone from the panchayat explained the scheme to you? (Yes/No)

5. How has village cleanliness changed in the last three years? (Improved a lot/Somewhat/No change/Worse)

They are short, simple, neutral and closed, ordered from general to specific, and avoid personal or leading questions.

Example: choosing tools for a study of migrant workers

A structured schedule for 300 workers (wages, hours, remittances) gives comparable numbers. Then a focus group discussion of 8 workers explores shared problems, life histories of 5 workers show how migration changed their families, a contractor and a union leader serve as key informants, and a month of non-participant observation at the work site checks what people say against what they do.

How to write the answer

Meaning → the tools asked, each with features, advantages and limitations → when to use which → conclusion.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up questionnaire and schedule.
  • Calling a focus group discussion just a 'group interview' — stress the moderator and group interaction.

Qualitative Research & Analysis

Moderate Asked in 6 of 8 papers · avg 9 marks

Qualitative vs quantitative research, qualitative research designs (phenomenology, ethnography, grounded theory, participatory action research, case study), qualitative data and sampling, and the approaches, techniques and steps of qualitative data analysis.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 4 · Unit 11 Analysis of Qualitative DataBlock 2 · Unit 5 Tools of Data Collection (5.2)

Key points

  • Qualitative research studies meanings, experiences and social processes in depth, usually with small purposive samples and words rather than numbers; quantitative research measures and tests hypotheses on large samples.
  • Differences: constructed and multiple realities vs a single reality; researcher involved vs detached; inductive vs deductive; small purposive vs large random samples; findings bound to context vs generalisable; words, observations and texts vs numbers.
  • Designs: phenomenology (people's lived experience, e.g. losing a job), ethnography ('portrait of a people' — long participant observation of a group's culture), grounded theory (Glaser and Strauss — theory built from the data through coding, memoing and constant comparison), participatory action research (the community studies and acts on its own problem with the researcher), case study.
  • Qualitative sampling: snowball (participants refer others), purposive, convenience, extreme/deviant case, homogeneous, maximum variation and opportunistic sampling.
  • Approaches to analysis: deductive (use a structure set in advance, e.g. the research questions — quick) or inductive (let themes emerge from the data — slower, used when little is known).
  • Techniques: content analysis (categorise and count words, themes), narrative analysis (reconstruct people's stories), discourse analysis (language in its context), framework analysis (familiarise → thematic framework → code → chart → map and interpret), grounded theory analysis, thematic analysis (identify recurring themes).
  • Steps in analysis: transcribe the data → organise it by research question → code it (descriptive, in-vivo, pattern codes) → validate (accuracy and reliability) → draw conclusions. Software: NVivo, ATLAS.ti, MAXQDA, HyperRESEARCH.
  • Epistemology is the theory of how we know — quantitative research assumes objective, value-free knowledge; qualitative research sees knowledge as built through interaction and interpretation.

Worked examples

Example: analysing interviews step by step

A researcher interviews 15 women in self-help groups about empowerment. Transcribe the recordings into text → organise the answers under each research question → code them ('own income', 'say in decisions', 'confidence to travel'; in-vivo codes like 'my own money') → group the codes into themes (economic, social, personal) → validate by checking with participants and a second coder → write conclusions with quotations.

Ideas emerging from the data (inductive) suit this kind of study because little was known beforehand.

Example: matching the design to the question

What is it like to lose one's land to a dam? → phenomenology. How does a fishing village organise its daily life? → ethnography (months of participant observation). Why do small traders adopt or reject UPI? → grounded theory (build a theory from coded interviews). Villagers and a researcher together study and fix their water problem → participatory action research. One cooperative dairy studied in depth → case study.

How to write the answer

Definition → comparison table or the designs/techniques asked → steps of analysis → a short example → conclusion.

Common mistakes

  • Describing qualitative analysis as 'no analysis, just quotes' — show the coding and theme steps.
  • Confusing ethnography (culture of a group) with phenomenology (individual lived experience).

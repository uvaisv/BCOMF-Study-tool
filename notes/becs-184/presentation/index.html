Classification, Tabulation, Diagrams & Graphs

Critical Asked in 7 of 8 papers · avg 18 marks

Objectives and types of classification, frequency distributions, tabulation and the parts of a table, bar diagrams, pie charts and pictograms, histogram, frequency polygon, ogives, importance and misuse of graphs.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 2 · Unit 6 Data Presentation

Key points

  • Classification groups data by common characteristics — it condenses data, shows similarities and differences, and makes comparison possible.
  • Types: simple array (data in ascending or descending order), discrete frequency distribution (each value with its frequency, using tally marks), grouped frequency distribution (exclusive classes like 10–20, 20–30 or inclusive classes like 10–19, 20–29) and cumulative ('less than' and 'more than') distributions; also classification by attribute, geography or time.
  • Tabulation presents classified data in rows and columns. Parts of a table: table number, title, caption (column headings), stub (row headings), body, head note (units), footnote and source.
  • Rules for a table: compact and clear, logical order, units stated, related items placed together, totals where useful, full source given.
  • Diagrams (bar, multiple bar, component bar, pie chart, pictogram) only display data; graphs (line graph, histogram, frequency polygon, frequency curve, ogive) can also be used for analysis.
  • Bar diagram vs histogram: bars have equal width and gaps and suit categories; a histogram's bars touch, the width is the class interval and the area shows frequency — it is for continuous grouped data.
  • Frequency polygon: join the mid-points of the histogram's tops (adding empty classes at both ends). Its advantages over a histogram: several distributions can be compared on one graph, and it shows the shape of the distribution more clearly.
  • Ogive: 'less than' cumulative frequencies against upper class limits (rising curve) and 'more than' against lower limits (falling curve); where they cross is the median. Graphs also show trends, help forecasting and reveal correlation (scatter diagram).
  • Misuse: a y-axis that does not start at zero, unequal scales or 3-D effects can exaggerate small differences.

Worked examples

Example: histogram and frequency polygon (Dec 2023 Q10)

Value 5–10, 10–15, 15–20, 20–25, 25–30 with frequencies 12, 25, 30, 40, 20.

Histogram: mark 5, 10, … 30 on the x-axis and frequency 0–40 on the y-axis; draw touching bars of heights 12, 25, 30, 40 and 20; title and label both axes. The tallest bar (20–25) is the modal class.

Frequency polygon: join the mid-points of the bar tops (7.5, 12.5, 17.5, 22.5, 27.5) and close at 2.5 and 32.5 on the x-axis.

Example: parts of a table

Table 3 (table number): Enrolment by Course and Gender, 2025 (title). Stub: courses (B.Com, BA, B.Sc) in rows. Caption: Male, Female, Total in columns. Head note: (in thousands). Body: the figures. Footnote: '*Provisional'. Source: University Annual Report 2025.

Diagrams

How to solve

  1. Histogram: classes on the x-axis (continuous, exclusive limits), frequency on the y-axis; draw touching bars; label axes and title.
  2. If class widths differ, plot frequency density (frequency ÷ width).
  3. Frequency polygon: mark mid-point of each bar top, join with straight lines, close at the mid-points of empty classes at each end.

How to write the answer

Meaning and purpose → types or parts (as asked) → a neat labelled diagram from given or hypothetical data → advantages/precautions → conclusion.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving gaps between histogram bars, or drawing a histogram for categories.
  • Not labelling axes, scale and title.
  • Plotting a 'less than' ogive against mid-points instead of upper class limits.

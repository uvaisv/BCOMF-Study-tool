Hypothesis Testing, z/t Tests & ANOVA

High Asked in 4 of 8 papers · avg 11 marks

Null and alternative hypotheses, one- and two-tailed tests, steps in testing, type I and II errors, level of significance and power, the p-value method, z and t tests, F-test for variances and ANOVA for three or more means.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 3 · Unit 7 Univariate Data Analysis (7.3.6)Block 3 · Unit 8 Bivariate Data Analysis (8.6 and ANOVA)

Key points

  • A statistical hypothesis is a claim about a population parameter. Null hypothesis H₀: no difference (contains '='); alternative H₁: there is a difference (≠ two-tailed; > right-tailed; < left-tailed).
  • Steps (traditional method): state H₀ and H₁ → choose the level of significance α and the test → find the critical value(s) → compute the test statistic from the sample → reject H₀ if it falls in the critical region → state the conclusion in words.
  • Type I error: rejecting a true H₀ (probability α). Type II error: not rejecting a false H₀ (probability β). Power = 1 − β. Reducing α raises β for a given sample; a larger sample reduces both.
  • Which is more serious depends on the case: convicting an innocent person or approving a harmful drug (type I) is usually treated as worse, so α is kept small (0.05 or 0.01).
  • p-value method: the p-value is the probability of getting a result at least as extreme as the sample's if H₀ is true. If p ≤ α reject H₀; if p > α do not reject.
  • z-test: population SD known, or large samples (n ≥ 30). t-test: population SD unknown and a small sample from a roughly normal population; d.f. = n − 1. Both compare one mean with a value, or two means; a z-test also compares two proportions.
  • F-test compares two variances (F = larger variance ÷ smaller variance).
  • ANOVA tests three or more means at once using F = between-group variance ÷ within-group variance. Repeated t-tests are avoided because they ignore the other means, inflate the chance of a type I error and need many tests (3 means → 3 tests, 5 → 10). Assumptions: normal populations, independent samples, equal variances.
  • MANOVA extends ANOVA to two or more dependent variables at once (e.g. effect of teaching method on both marks and attendance).

Worked examples

Example: a one-sample test

A battery maker claims a mean life of 36 months. A sample of 36 batteries with an additive averages 37.5 months; σ = 3 months.

H₀: μ = 36; H₁: μ > 36 (right-tailed), α = 0.05, critical z = 1.645.

z = (37.5 − 36) ÷ (3/√36) = 1.5 ÷ 0.5 = 3.0 > 1.645 → reject H₀: the additive increases battery life. Since z = 3 has a p-value of about 0.001 < 0.05, the p-value method gives the same decision.

Example: types of error and ANOVA

Type I: a court convicts an innocent person (H₀ 'innocent' rejected though true). Type II: a guilty person goes free. A drug regulator keeps α small because approving a useless drug (type I) is very costly.

To compare mean yields of three fertilisers, use one ANOVA F-test (between-group variance ÷ within-group variance) instead of three t-tests, which would raise the overall chance of a type I error.

How to write the answer

Definitions → steps or comparison as headed points → a short worked illustration (H₀, H₁, decision) → conclusion.

Common mistakes

  • Writing 'accept H₀' as proof that it is true — say 'do not reject'.
  • Putting the '=' sign in H₁.
  • Using many t-tests instead of ANOVA for three or more groups.

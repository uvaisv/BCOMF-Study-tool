Correlation & Regression (Bivariate Analysis)

Critical Asked in 7 of 8 papers · avg 18 marks

Bivariate analysis, scatter plots, Pearson's correlation coefficient and its significance test, correlation vs causation, regression lines and coefficients, coefficient of determination and standard error of estimate.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 3 · Unit 8 Bivariate Data Analysis

Key points

  • Bivariate analysis studies the relationship between two variables (advertising and sales, study hours and marks); start with a scatter plot.
  • Pearson's product-moment correlation coefficient r measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship; −1 ≤ r ≤ +1; r = 0 means no linear relation. It is independent of origin and scale.
  • Test of significance: H₀: ρ = 0 vs H₁: ρ ≠ 0; t = r√(n − 2) ÷ √(1 − r²) with n − 2 degrees of freedom; reject H₀ if |t| exceeds the critical value.
  • Correlation is not causation. A significant r may come from: X causing Y, Y causing X (reverse), a third (lurking) variable causing both, a complex set of many variables, or coincidence. Averages can exaggerate correlation.
  • To study cause and effect: plan an experiment or control the other factors, check the time order (the cause comes first), rule out third variables, and use regression with the cause as the independent variable.
  • Regression estimates one variable from another. Y on X: Y = a + bX, found by least squares (minimise the sum of squared vertical errors) from the normal equations ΣY = na + bΣX and ΣXY = aΣX + bΣX².
  • Properties of regression coefficients: both have the same sign as r; r = ±√(b_yx × b_xy) (geometric mean); if one exceeds 1 the other is below 1; independent of origin but not of scale; both lines pass through (X̄, Ȳ); r = ±1 → lines coincide, r = 0 → lines perpendicular.
  • Coefficient of determination r² = explained variation ÷ total variation — the share of the change in Y explained by X (r = 0.9 → 81%).
  • Standard error of estimate = √[Σ(Y − Ŷ)² ÷ (n − 2)] — the spread of actual values around the regression line; smaller means better predictions.

Worked examples

Example: correlation and its significance (Dec 2022, Q2)

Cars X: 63.0, 29.0, 20.8, 19.1, 13.4, 8.5; revenue Y: 7.0, 3.9, 2.1, 2.8, 1.4, 1.5 (n = 6).

ΣX = 153.8, ΣY = 18.7, ΣXY = 682.77, ΣX² = 5,859.26, ΣY² = 80.67.

r = (6 × 682.77 − 153.8 × 18.7) ÷ √[(6 × 5,859.26 − 153.8²)(6 × 80.67 − 18.7²)] = 1,220.56 ÷ 1,242.96 = 0.982.

t = r√(n − 2) ÷ √(1 − r²) = 0.982 × 2 ÷ √(1 − 0.964) = 10.39 > 2.776 (critical value, 4 d.f.) → reject H₀: the correlation is significant.

Example: correlation vs causation, and r²

Ice-cream sales and drowning deaths are highly correlated, but both are caused by hot weather (a lurking third variable). To test whether a smart board raises marks, compare a class taught with it and a similar class without it, before and after (control other factors), then test the difference in means.

If r = 0.9 between advertising and sales, r² = 0.81: 81% of the variation in sales is explained by advertising, and 19% by other factors.

How to solve

  1. Table of X, Y, X², Y², XY; find the totals.
  2. r = [nΣXY − ΣXΣY] ÷ √{[nΣX² − (ΣX)²][nΣY² − (ΣY)²]}.
  3. Significance: t = r√(n − 2)/√(1 − r²); compare with the critical t at n − 2 d.f.
  4. Regression Y on X: b = [nΣXY − ΣXΣY] ÷ [nΣX² − (ΣX)²]; a = Ȳ − bX̄.

Formulas

r = [nΣXY − ΣXΣY] ÷ √{[nΣX² − (ΣX)²][nΣY² − (ΣY)²]}
t = r√(n − 2) ÷ √(1 − r²)
b = [nΣXY − ΣXΣY] ÷ [nΣX² − (ΣX)²], a = Ȳ − bX̄
r² = Explained variation ÷ Total variation

How to write the answer

Meaning → formula and working (for sums) or properties as headed points → interpretation in words → conclusion.

Common mistakes

  • Concluding causation from a high r.
  • Using regression to predict when r is not significant.
  • Mixing up b_yx (Y on X) and b_xy (X on Y).

Communication in NGOs

Critical Asked in 11 of 12 papers · avg 10 marks

Principles and process of communication, human communication components, verbal and non-verbal communication, group and face-to-face communication, feedback, barriers, and social factors affecting communication.

In your IGNOU bookUnit 8 Importance and Scope of Communication SkillsUnit 9 Interpersonal and Group Communication

Key points

  • Human communication: sender, message, encoding, channel, receiver, decoding, feedback, noise and context.
  • Principles (7 Cs): clear, concise, concrete, correct, coherent, complete, courteous; also know the audience, use the right channel, seek feedback.
  • Verbal communication: spoken or written words — face-to-face, meetings, phone, letters, reports. Effective verbal messages are clear, simple, relevant, well-timed and suited to the audience.
  • Non-verbal communication: gestures, facial expressions, eye contact, posture, tone, dress and space — vital for NGO workers in communities where literacy is low.
  • Two-way communication and feedback: feedback confirms understanding and builds trust with communities and donors. Barriers to giving and receiving feedback: fear, defensiveness, status differences, poor listening, timing.
  • Group communication: discussion within a group for shared decisions — used in self-help groups and community meetings. Face-to-face communication: most personal and persuasive.
  • Barriers: physical (noise, distance), language and semantic, psychological (attitudes, prejudice), social and cultural (caste, class, gender, age), organisational. Social sets like caste, class, gender and age shape who speaks, who is heard and how messages are received.
  • Overcoming barriers: local language, simple messages, visual aids, trust-building, active listening, involving community leaders.
  • Models of communication grew from Aristotle's 'art of persuasion' (speaker-focused) to two-way models where each person is both source and receiver.
  • Personal space (proxemics): intimate, personal, social (about 4–12 feet — official discussions) and public distance (beyond 12 feet — lectures, meetings).
  • Listening barriers in the book: second-guessing the speaker, stereotyping, the halo effect (judging a person by one good trait), and not listening because of status or gender.

Worked example

Example: overcoming barriers in the field

A health NGO's printed posters on safe drinking water fail in a village where many women cannot read. The team switches to a street play in the local dialect, uses pictures, sits with women in their own courtyards (personal distance, not a raised stage), and asks them to repeat the key steps back — feedback that confirms understanding.

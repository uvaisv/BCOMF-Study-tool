Personal Selling: Concept & Situations

Critical Asked in 6 of 9 papers · avg 13 marks

Meaning, nature, characteristics and objectives of personal selling; selling situations; selling vs marketing.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 1 · Unit 1 Introduction to Personal SellingBlock 1 · Unit 3 Sales Management (3.7 Selling vs marketing)

Key points

  • Personal selling is the part of the promotion mix where a salesperson deals with the customer personally — face to face or online — in direct two-way communication, using words and body language. It is a 'push' strategy.
  • Characteristics: flexible (presentation fits the customer), engaging, builds relationships, quick response and feedback.
  • Use it for B2B selling, high-priced or technical products, new products that need explaining, a few large customers, serving middlemen, or when the advertising budget is small.
  • Advantages: two-way and flexible, sharp focus on target customers, personal attention, instant feedback, measurable results, extra non-selling tasks. Disadvantages: high cost, high staff turnover, message not standardised, salespeople differ in ability and motivation.
  • Qualitative objectives: find prospects, stimulate demand, serve and keep customers, collect market information, support middlemen, build brand image and goodwill. Quantitative (short-term): sales volume, product mix, expense limits, market share, number of new customers.
  • Selling functions: delivery salesperson, inside and outside order-takers, missionary salespeople, support staff (technical, merchandisers, customer service) and creative salespeople (order-getters).
  • Process: prospecting → preparation → approach → presentation → handling objections → closing → follow-up.
  • Selling vs marketing: selling is part of marketing — exchanging goods for a price; marketing starts with finding customer needs and ends with customer satisfaction and feedback.

Worked examples

Example: when personal selling is the right tool

A company making hospital ventilators sells to a few large hospitals. The product is costly and technical, and doctors have questions. A trained salesperson visits, demonstrates the machine, answers doubts on the spot, adjusts the offer (training, service contract) and follows up after installation. TV advertising would be wasteful here; two-way, face-to-face selling fits.

Example: order-taker vs order-getter

A salesperson who visits grocery shops every week to take repeat orders for biscuits is an order-taker. A salesperson who persuades a college to buy a new learning-management software it had never considered is a creative salesperson (order-getter).

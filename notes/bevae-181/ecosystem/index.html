Ecosystem Structure, Energy Flow & Cycles

Critical Asked in 11 of 11 papers · avg 5 questions

What an ecosystem is, its living and non-living parts, trophic levels and food chains, energy flow, nutrient cycles and ecological succession.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 1 · Unit 2 Ecosystems

Key points

  • An ecosystem is the basic functional unit of nature: organisms interacting with each other and with their physical surroundings. It has no fixed size or boundary and regulates itself through feedback. The term was coined by Tansley.
  • Biosphere: the zone of land (lithosphere), water (hydrosphere) and air (atmosphere) where life exists — from about 11,000 m below the ocean surface to about 9,000 m above mean sea level. Life is abundant from about 200 m below sea level to about 6,000 m above.
  • Abiotic components: light, temperature, rainfall, water, nutrients. Biotic: producers (autotrophs), consumers (heterotrophs or phagotrophs) and decomposers (saprotrophs or reducers — mainly bacteria and fungi). Chemosynthetic bacteria are autotrophs.
  • Trophic levels: I producers → II herbivores (primary consumers) → III carnivores (secondary) → IV tertiary consumers → V top carnivores (quaternary). Tigers, lions and vultures are top carnivores; humans, as omnivores, sit at several levels.
  • Food chains: grazing (starts with green plants), detritus (starts with dead, decaying organic matter) and parasitic (e.g. goat → nematodes → bacteria). Linked food chains form a food web.
  • Energy flows one way through an ecosystem and is lost as heat at each level, so ecosystems are open systems that need a constant input of solar energy. The sun also drives the water cycle.
  • Nutrient cycles are gaseous (reservoir: atmosphere or hydrosphere — water, carbon, nitrogen) or sedimentary (reservoir: earth's crust — phosphorus, calcium). The sulphur cycle is mostly sedimentary with a short gaseous phase; its large reservoir is soil and sediments. Nitrogen's main reservoir is the atmosphere.
  • Ecological succession: lichens form the pioneer community on bare rock, followed by mosses, grasses, shrubs and trees. Each intermediate stage is a sere (seral stage); the final, stable stage is the climax community.
  • Primary succession starts on an area that never had life; secondary succession follows the destruction of an existing community.

Worked examples

Example: a pond as an ecosystem

Abiotic: water, sunlight, dissolved oxygen and nutrients. Producers: phytoplankton and water plants. Primary consumers: zooplankton and small fish that eat them. Secondary consumers: larger fish. Top consumers: kingfishers. Decomposers: bacteria and fungi on the bottom, returning nutrients to the water.

Food chain: phytoplankton → zooplankton → small fish → large fish → kingfisher.

Example: energy loss and succession

Only about 10% of energy passes from one trophic level to the next: 10,000 units in plants → 1,000 in herbivores → 100 in carnivores → 10 in top carnivores. This is why food chains are short and top predators are few.

On a bare rock, lichens come first, then mosses, grasses, shrubs and finally trees — primary succession ending in a climax forest.

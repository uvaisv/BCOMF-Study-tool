Environment & Human Health

Low Asked in 10 of 11 papers · avg 1 questions

Health and public health, the genetic, behavioural and environmental influences on health, and the vocabulary of disease.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 4 · Unit 13 Human Communities and Environment (13.3 Human health and welfare)

Key points

  • WHO defines health as complete physical, mental and social well-being, not merely the absence of disease.
  • Environmental health is the part of public health concerned with all external conditions that affect health. Public health as a discipline began in Europe after 1842 ('the great sanitary awakening') and is now called community health: the patient represents the community.
  • Aetiology is the study of the cause or origin of disease; the host is the organism a parasite enters and the pathogen is the parasite that causes disease.
  • Health depends on genetic, behavioural and environmental influences.
  • Hereditary (genetic) diseases: phenylketonuria, haemophilia, mongolism (Down's syndrome), sickle-cell anaemia, thalassaemia. Allergies, diabetes, hypertension and schizophrenia are not entirely genetic — they come from genes interacting with the environment. Malaria is infectious, not genetic.
  • Behavioural influences: smoking, alcoholism, drugs, chewing tobacco and irregular eating.
  • Environmental influences — physical: radiation, noise, vibration, humidity; chemical: poisons and toxins; biological: microbes, animals, allergens; sociological: overcrowding, isolation, neglect; psychological: stress, anxiety, depression, boredom.

Worked examples

Examples: environmental influences on health

Physical: long exposure to traffic noise causes stress and hearing loss. Chemical: arsenic in groundwater in parts of West Bengal causes skin lesions and cancer. Biological: stagnant water breeds mosquitoes that spread malaria and dengue. Sociological: overcrowded slums spread tuberculosis. Psychological: constant work stress leads to anxiety and depression.

Example: genes vs environment

Thalassaemia and haemophilia are hereditary — passed on through genes. Type 2 diabetes and hypertension come from genes interacting with lifestyle and environment: an inactive life, unhealthy food and stress raise the risk even in people with a family history.

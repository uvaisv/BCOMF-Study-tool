Environmental Movements, Ethics & Justice

High Asked in 11 of 11 papers · avg 3 questions

The three views of nature, attitudes towards nature, why we need environmental ethics, equity, justice and racism, and India's environmental movements.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 4 · Unit 14 Environmental EthicsBlock 4 · Unit 13 Human Communities and Environment (13.7 People's movements)

Key points

  • Three views of nature: Western (European and North American) — nature is alien and hostile, to be conquered; Sinaitic (Chinese, Korean, Japanese) — nature is beautiful and humans are part of it; Indian (Hindu, Buddhist and Jain) — nature is a mother and a goddess who cannot be tamed.
  • Attitudes: anthropocentrism puts far greater value on humans than on anything else; stewardship is a strong sense of responsibility for a particular part of nature — strong in tribal, pastoral and traditional farming communities, weak in urban ones.
  • Ecofeminism was proposed as an alternative to patriarchal systems of domination. Biocentrism holds that all living organisms have values and rights whether or not they are useful; ecocentrism extends rights to ecosystems and non-living parts of nature. Aldo Leopold wrote the essay 'The Land Ethic'.
  • A new set of environmental ethics is needed because of new effects on nature, new knowledge about nature and expanding moral concerns.
  • Environmental equity has three categories: procedural (unfair procedures — packing boards with business interests, holding hearings in remote places, English-only material for non-English speakers); geographical (communities hosting waste sites get fewer benefits than those producing the waste); social (decisions that mirror power and racial bias, e.g. against certain ethnic groups).
  • Environmental justice means equal protection under environmental laws for everyone regardless of race, ethnicity or income; environmental racism is the placing of hazards on disadvantaged communities.
  • Religions teach us to be trustees or stewards of the environment, not its masters. Environmental education and organised collective action (student groups, campaigns) change attitudes.
  • Movements and cases: Chipko (Chandi Prasad Bhatt), Silent Valley (the valley of the Kunthi river, Kerala), Narmada Bachao Andolan, and the Taj Trapezium case (Agra's brick kilns, the Mathura refinery and Firozabad's glass factories).

Worked examples

Example: Chipko and Silent Valley

Chipko (1970s, Garhwal Himalaya): when contractors came to fell trees, village women, led at Reni by Gaura Devi, hugged the trees to protect them. Their protest led to a ban on commercial felling in the area.

Silent Valley (Kerala): a proposed hydroelectric dam on the Kunthi river would have flooded rare rain forest. After a long campaign by scientists and citizens, the project was dropped and the area became a national park.

Example: environmental inequity

A city's garbage comes mostly from well-off areas, but the dump is placed next to a poor settlement — geographical inequity. If the public hearing on it is held far away and only in English, that is procedural inequity too.

Global Issues: Climate, Ozone, Acid Rain & Disasters

Critical Asked in 11 of 11 papers · avg 8 questions

The greenhouse effect and climate change with the treaties and India's action plan, ozone depletion, acid rain, and natural disasters.

In your IGNOU bookBlock 3 · Unit 11 Global Environmental IssuesBlock 4 · Unit 13 Human Communities and Environment (13.4–13.5 Natural disasters)

Key points

  • Greenhouse gases (carbon dioxide, methane, ozone, CFCs, water vapour) trap outgoing infrared heat and keep the earth at about +15°C instead of −18°C. Water vapour causes about two-thirds of the effect and CO₂ about a quarter; nitrogen, oxygen and argon do not absorb infrared. Sulphur dioxide is not a greenhouse gas.
  • Of incoming solar radiation, about a third is reflected, about 20% is absorbed by atmospheric gases and the rest by the surface.
  • Global warming effects: sea-level rise (warming water expands and ice melts), more floods and droughts, farm and forest losses, and more malaria, dengue and yellow fever.
  • Climate treaties: the IPCC was set up in 1988 by WMO and UNEP; UNFCCC was adopted at Rio in 1992 and came into force in 1994; the Kyoto Protocol (1997) required developed countries to cut greenhouse gases 5.2% below 1990 levels; the Paris Agreement (COP21, 2015) aims to keep warming well below 2°C.
  • India's National Action Plan on Climate Change (2008) has 8 missions: solar, enhanced energy efficiency, sustainable habitat, water, sustaining the Himalayan ecosystem, Green India, sustainable agriculture, and strategic knowledge for climate change.
  • The ozone layer lies in the stratosphere (15–50 km), is measured in Dobson units and absorbs about 99% of UV. UVA is least energetic and not absorbed; most UVB is absorbed (2–3% reaches us) and causes skin cancer and cataract; UVC is absorbed by oxygen and ozone.
  • CFCs (used in sprays, deodorants, paints, refrigeration) are stable, rise to the stratosphere, and are broken by UV, releasing chlorine that destroys ozone. The Vienna Convention (1985) led to the Montreal Protocol (16 September 1987) — 16 September is Ozone Day.
  • Acid rain comes from sulphur and nitrogen oxides (not CFCs). Unpolluted rain has a pH of about 5.7; precipitation below 5.7 counts as acid deposition. It harms crops, forests, aquatic life and buildings, and causes heart and lung problems.
  • Disaster types: atmospheric (heat waves, cyclones — which form over tropical seas warmer than about 27°C), geological (earthquakes, landslides, avalanches), hydrological (floods, drought) and biological (pest invasions). Asia is the region most prone to natural calamities.
  • Tsunamis are seismic sea waves; the Indian coast's most devastating one was in 2004, and mangroves are a natural defence. Zone V is India's most severe seismic zone (very high damage risk); the Rann of Kachchh is in a high zone.

Worked examples

Example: the greenhouse effect in a parked car

A car left in the sun with its windows shut gets much hotter inside than outside: sunlight enters through the glass, but the heat given off inside cannot escape easily. Greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide and water vapour do the same for the earth — keeping it at about +15°C rather than −18°C. Adding more of them traps more heat and causes global warming.

Examples: ozone, acid rain and disasters in India

Ozone: CFCs from old refrigerators and sprays drift up to the stratosphere, where UV light frees chlorine that breaks down ozone — the reason the Montreal Protocol phased CFCs out.

Acid rain: sulphur dioxide from industry around Agra was linked to the yellowing of the Taj Mahal's marble, leading to the Taj Trapezium case.

Disasters: in the 2004 tsunami, stretches of the Tamil Nadu coast behind dense mangroves suffered less damage — mangroves act as a natural barrier.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing the Montreal Protocol (ozone) with the Kyoto Protocol (greenhouse gases).
